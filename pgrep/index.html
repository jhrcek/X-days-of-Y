9 Daysofpgrep

From “it is ps aux pipe grep, with fewer keystrokes” to querying the process table by cgroup, namespace and environment, and knowing exactly which of the three verbs — list, signal, wait — you are about to apply to the result. One sitting a day, each small enough to actually use at work the same afternoon.

How to use this

One sitting a day

Each lesson introduces a handful of criteria and stops. The drills at the bottom are the actual lesson — pgrep is a habit rather than a body of knowledge, and reading about -f teaches you nothing. Do them against the processes already running on your own machine.

Type it before you need it

The day you reach for pgrep in earnest is a day something is wrong and you are in a hurry. Use it for trivial lookups from Day 1 — pgrep -a instead of ps aux | grep, every time — so that the muscle memory is there when the stakes are not trivial.

Build a recipes file

pgrep has no configuration file, no dotfile and no environment variables: there is nothing to tune and nothing to get wrong. What you accumulate instead is a file of queries. Each day ends with a line or two for ~/pgrep-recipes.sh, always with the reason as a comment, and Day 9 asks you to delete every line you can no longer defend.

What is deliberately left out

Signal semantics beyond what pkill needs — signal(7) is the page for that, and Day 9 points at it. The regular-expression language itself is used throughout but taught nowhere; regex(7) already does it better. Nothing here covers reading process state — memory, CPU, scheduling — which is ps(1) and htop(1) territory and has its own course in this repository. And the BSD and Solaris behaviours the portable documentation hints at are ignored entirely: this is procps-ng on Linux, checked against the binary.
